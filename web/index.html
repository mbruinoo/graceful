Sliding Brackets

An unsolved puzzle about trees, redrawn so you can push it around with your hands. Each section adds one idea.

The puzzle

A tree is a set of dots joined by lines, with no loops. Number the dots 1, 2, 3, … and write on each line the difference between the numbers at its two ends.

The numbering is called graceful if the differences are all different. With 6 dots there are 5 lines, so the differences must be exactly 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5.

A graceful numbering of a tree with 6 dots. The orange numbers are the differences: each of 1 to 5 appears once.

The puzzle: can every tree be numbered gracefully? It was asked in the 1960s. Computers have confirmed it for every tree with up to 35 dots. Nobody has proved it, and nothing on this page does either.

Put the numbers on a line

Here is the same tree, drawn differently. The numbers sit in order along a line. Each line of the tree becomes a bracket joining its two numbers, so the bracket’s length is the difference.

“All differences different” now means: one bracket of each length. That is why they stack so neatly. Each length gets its own height, longest on top.

Brackets slide, and not every position gives a tree

A bracket can slide left or right at its own height. Wherever the brackets sit, there is still one of each length, so the differences are still all different.

But the brackets do not always form a tree. Drag one below and watch. When some brackets close a loop they turn red, and a number is left with nothing attached. A green arrow marks a one-step slide that keeps the picture a tree.

Every tree shape should turn up

So the puzzle becomes: among all the positions that do give a tree, does every possible tree shape appear?

For small trees you can see that it does. Pick a shape and the brackets show one position that makes it.

The number of shapes grows quickly: 6 shapes with 6 dots, 106 with 10, over a hundred million with 25. The rest of this page is about ways of finding the brackets for a given shape without trying everything.

First idea: start from the star and slide

Push every bracket as far left as it goes. All of them then stand on number 1, which makes a star: one dot joined to all the others.

From the star, slide one bracket one step to the right, then another, keeping a tree at every step. Each slide changes the shape a little. Pick a tree and watch the shortest route to it.

For every tree with up to 14 dots, a computer search found such a route. Whether one always exists is not known. It is now proved for one family beyond the simplest: every spider (a star with lengthened arms) whose arms have length at most 2.

A family where it always works

Here is a recipe that cannot fail. Place the longest bracket. Then, for each shorter bracket in turn, keep one foot of the bracket before it and move the other foot one step inward. You only choose which foot to keep.

The brackets nest like a staircase, so call this a staircase. The trees it makes are the caterpillars: a path with single dots hanging off it.

Why it is always a tree. The moving foot always lands on a number no earlier bracket has touched. So every bracket brings in exactly one new number, and a loop can never close.

Why caterpillars, and all of them. The feet that are kept form the path. Every other number was touched once, so it hangs off the path. Keeping the same side several times hangs several dots on one path dot; switching sides moves along the path. Any caterpillar can be dialled in. This is Alexander Rosa’s construction from 1967.

Sliding reaches all of them. Starting from the star and sliding right one step at a time, you can reach every staircase while staying a staircase throughout.

A second picture of the same thing, and why nothing else behaves this way

Draw each bracket as one cell of a grid: its row is the left foot, its column is the right foot. A staircase is a path of cells that only steps down or right. Change the choices above and the path changes.

Only staircases work like this. Suppose every bracket jumps over one common point of the line, and the brackets, placed longest first, each bring in exactly one new number. Then the picture must be a staircase. Read the grid backwards: at the end the used rows and columns fill a rectangle, and the last cell has to be its far corner. Remove it and the same is true one step earlier. So the cells form a down-or-right path. A tree that is not a caterpillar needs at least one bracket that does not jump the common point.

Second idea: build the tree longest bracket first

The staircase worked because each new bracket brought in exactly one new number. Keep only that rule. Place the brackets from longest to shortest, anywhere, as long as each one stands with one foot on a number already used and the other on a fresh number.

The tree then grows one dot at a time, and a loop can never close. Pick a tree and step through one way of growing it.

A computer search found such a build for every tree with up to 26 dots, which is 279,793,450 trees at 26 alone. Whether one always exists is not known. If it does, the original puzzle is solved.

The same rule, said two other ways

Downhill from one dot. Hold the finished tree by the dot numbered 1. The build was longest-first exactly when the differences get strictly smaller along every path leading away from that dot.

Without mentioning lengths. Look only at which numbers are used. A fresh number may join a used number exactly when the used numbers strictly between them are as many as the fresh numbers outside them. Playing by that rule from the two end numbers gives exactly these builds.

What the build looks like: two blocks and a few skips

Under the brackets in the last figure is a tape: one row per step, one cell per number. Blue cells are numbers already in the tree and the orange cell is the one just added.

The tape almost always shows the same thing. A block of used numbers grows inward from each end. Now and then a number is skipped, and filled in later. Here is a tree that needs one skip. Step through and watch for it.

How many skipped numbers must be allowed at once?

Skips allowed at one timeSmallest tree that cannot be built
none9 dots
one10 dots
two22 dots (one tree out of 5,623,756)

So no small fixed number of skips is enough. The number needed creeps up as trees get larger. Each time a tighter rule looked universal, a bigger tree broke it. That is worth remembering about every “checked up to” on this page.

What a skip does can be said exactly. With no skips, each new number joins the innermost number of the opposite block. That is a staircase step: the build walks down one path and can never return to a dot it has passed. With one skip waiting, each new number joins the number one place further in. With two, two places in. So a skip brings a dot that was already passed back into reach.

In the example above, after 1 and 9 are joined and 2 is hung on 9, the centre (number 1) is buried under 2. Skipping 8 is what lets 7 and 6 join the centre and start two more legs. Held steady, one skip lets three paths grow in rotation, and two skips let five.

What is settled and what is not

Proved on this page

  • Staircases always give caterpillars, and give every caterpillar. (Rosa’s result, in this picture.)
  • Sliding right from the star reaches every caterpillar, and every spider whose arms have length at most 2.
  • If every bracket jumps one common point and the build is longest-first, it is a staircase.
  • A new number joins the number as many places in from the far block’s tip as there are skips waiting.
  • If two trees can each be built longest-first from a chosen dot, so can the first tree with an odd bunch of copies of the second attached at that dot (2m+1 copies, where m is the number of lines in the first). This gives infinitely many new buildable trees with no search; details in the README.

Shown to be false

  • That the build never needs more than two skips. The first exception has 22 dots.
  • That the build can start from any dot with four or more neighbours. The first exception has 24 dots.
  • That trees can be grown by opening a bounded number of gaps at a time.

Known before this page

  • The picture of one bracket per length (Sheppard, 1976; Haviar and Ivaška, 2015).
  • Building longest-first, as a way of searching by computer (Horton, 2003; Fang, 2010). Fang’s search confirmed the puzzle for every tree with up to 35 dots.

Still open

  • The puzzle itself, known as the Graceful Tree Conjecture.
  • Whether sliding from the star, or building longest-first, always works.

Further results in the README, each checked by two independent referees: explicit builds for several infinite families of trees with four-step diameter, exact arithmetic rules for which dot a build can start from, and an independent re-computation of the numbers on this page.
