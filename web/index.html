Graceful Tree Conjecture · a bracket model

Every tree, slid out of a star?

Put the numbers 1 to n on a line. Give each length 1, 2, …, n−1 its own rail and one bracket of that length. Wherever the brackets sit, the picture is a graceful labeling of something. The conjecture says every tree shape can be made this way.

The conjecture is still open, and nothing here proves it. What follows is the model made exact, two small theorems with proofs you can watch, and two stronger conjectures that a computer search supports for every tree it could reach.

The bench

Drag a bracket along its rail, or tap a green arrow. Arrows appear only where a one-step slide keeps the picture a tree. Brackets that close a loop turn red.

The slide rule

Proved

Lift one bracket off a tree and the tree falls into two pieces, one holding each foot. Set the bracket down one step over. The result is a tree exactly when its two feet land in different pieces.

Why. A tree on n vertices is n−1 edges with no loop. Removing an edge leaves two connected pieces. A new edge with one foot in each piece reconnects them without a loop. A new edge with both feet in the same piece closes a loop there and leaves the other piece cut off.

This is what the green arrows on the bench compute. A bracket whose far foot is a leaf can almost never slide, because the cut-off piece is that single vertex and the new feet must land on it.

Staircases are caterpillars

Proved

Place brackets longest first. Let each bracket keep one foot of the bracket before it and step the other foot one place inward. Choose which foot to keep at every step.

It is always a tree. The kept feet only move inward, so the foot that steps always lands on a vertex no longer bracket has touched. Every bracket adds exactly one new vertex, so no loop can close.

It is always a caterpillar, and every caterpillar appears. The kept feet form a path, the spine. Every other vertex was touched once, by a bracket hanging off the spine, so it is a leaf. A run of “keep left” hangs that many leaves on one spine vertex, and switching sides moves to the next spine vertex, so any list of leaf counts along a spine can be dialled in. This is Rosa’s 1967 labeling of caterpillars.

Right slides reach all of them. Sliding bracket k one step right turns “k keeps left, k−1 keeps right” into “k keeps right, k−1 keeps left”. Sliding bracket 1 right turns its “left” into “right”. So a “right” can be created at the shortest bracket and passed upward to any rail, one slide at a time, and every picture on the way is a staircase. Starting from the star (all “left”), every staircase is reached using right slides only.

Atlas of slides

Conjecture

Right-slide conjecture. Every tree can be reached from the star by sliding brackets one step to the right, one at a time, with a tree at every step. Since every picture on the bench is graceful, this would imply the Graceful Tree Conjecture.

It holds for all trees with at most 14 vertices. Pick a tree and watch the shortest route, or switch to the growth view described in the next section.

Growing longest-first

Conjecture

The staircase proof worked because each new bracket touched exactly one new vertex. Keep that property and drop the rest: place brackets n−1, n−2, …, 1 in order, anywhere on their rails, so that each one stands with one foot on a vertex already used and the other on a fresh vertex. Call this a growth placement. It is a tree for the same reason a staircase is.

Growth conjecture. Every tree has a growth placement. Read backwards: every tree has a graceful labeling in which edge 1 is a leaf edge, and after removing it edge 2 is a leaf edge, and so on up to n−1.

This asks for much more than gracefulness, yet it holds for every tree with at most 22 vertices, checked exhaustively, and for every larger random or structured tree tried, up to 50 vertices. The “Grow longest-first” view in the atlas shows one for each tree.

Why it is interesting. It turns the conjecture into a statement about building a tree leaf by leaf, which is the shape induction wants. At each moment the state is small: which numbers are used, and which vertices still have unbuilt branches. For the first half of the build the used numbers sit in two blocks at the ends of the line and every bracket jumps from one block to the other.

What is proved about it. Caterpillars have growth placements (the staircases). If a tree has a growth placement, so does the tree with one more leaf attached to either foot of its longest bracket: shift everything one place and add the new longest bracket on top.

What the search found

  • From 7 vertices on, one-step slides do not connect all graceful trees. Small islands split off. The star’s island holds 95–97% of placements and contains every tree shape.
  • No graceful tree is frozen: in every case checked, at least one bracket can slide one step.
  • If a bracket may jump any distance along its rail, all graceful trees on up to 14 vertices form a single island.
  • Slides that also keep the growth property are too rigid: from the star they reach exactly 5·2n−5−1 placements for 5 ≤ n ≤ 11 and miss some trees from 9 vertices on.

Where this stands

  • Proved here: the slide rule, the staircase theorem with its right-slide route, and the leaf-extension step for growth placements. All three are short, and the staircase labeling itself is classical.
  • Not proved: the Graceful Tree Conjecture, the right-slide conjecture, the growth conjecture. Each of the last two implies the first.
  • Related work: the “transfer” method of Hrnčiar and Haviar, used to show trees of diameter at most 5 are graceful, moves leaves between vertices of a labeled star. In this picture a transfer is a pair of brackets reflected together. A literature search did not turn up the slide graph or the growth property, but that search was brief and the claim of novelty should be checked by someone who knows the field.
  • Evidence is not proof. Graceful labelings are plentiful for small trees, so strong statements can survive small cases and still fail later.
